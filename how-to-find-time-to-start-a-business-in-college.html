How to find time to start a business in college

I started Lucid Bots as a Davidson College junior with $42 in the company account. This is the short version of what my book, The Student Founder, says about it.

By Andrew Ashur, founder and CEO of Lucid Bots · November 19, 2026

Short answer

In my experience a time problem is almost always a priority problem with an energy problem underneath it. Decide on Sunday what each hour of the coming week is for, give your sharpest hours to the hardest work, and when the week breaks, cut polish before you cut a single customer conversation.

In college I played four baseball games a week. On days without a game I had practice from four-thirty to seven, and most mornings I had lifts at six. I carried a double major and extra courses to graduate a year early, worked three part-time jobs, kept a long-distance relationship with Ana, and built the drone company that became Lucid Bots. Road weekends left Thursday afternoon and got home late Sunday night. My coursework was finished before the bus pulled out, so I spent those six hours on the bus researching the market and working on the technology. Everyone on my hall had the same 168 hours I did.

A weekly budget

The book’s budget runs on about fifteen hours a week, roughly what a student working thirty hours to make rent has left. Copy it this way:

  • Ninety minutes on Sunday: thirty to block out the week, an hour on the most important task.
  • Five two-hour blocks wherever your shifts allow, spent only on customer conversations, the ugly prototype or the next post.
  • One three-hour block on your day off for deep work.

If you only have four hours, the week is the Sunday ninety minutes plus one two-hour block. When something has to give, cut polish first, then scope, then pace. Never cut the customer conversations, because those hours can’t be replaced.

Win the morning

I woke while campus was still dark, often two or three hours before anyone else, and spent the first hours on the hardest problem I had. At ten most nights I turned off the light while people down the hall brewed coffee. The few nights I pulled an all-nighter, I thought less clearly for days. My sharpest hours came right after waking. If yours fall somewhere else, only the clock changes. Name your two sharpest hours and look at what is scheduled inside them this week.

Sort tasks by energy

I think about energy the way I thought about my pitching arm. You get a limited number of high-quality hours each day, so sort the task list before you plan the week:

  • High energy, sharp focus: the hardest work, like building the product or writing a pitch.
  • Energy, scattered focus: a run, a lift or a walk.
  • Tired and forcing deep work: stop and sleep.
  • Empty: email, scheduling and errands.

Work that took me two hours to grind out at eleven at night took twenty rested minutes the next morning.

Script the week on Sunday

Every Sunday I took thirty minutes, laid my syllabi, my baseball travel schedule and my startup to-do list side by side, and blocked the week down to the hour. On Monday I followed the script instead of deciding what to do. Treat each block as an appointment. If something has to move a block, find it another slot that same week before you agree, and if no slot exists, say no.

Stay a week ahead of the syllabus. If you start behind, spend one day off reading every syllabus and listing every deliverable due in the next fourteen days, then do the second week’s work first. When an essay was due, I wrote a fast draft a week early and took it to the professor, and every paper I ran that way came back better.

For one week, write a digit from one to four at the top of each block for the state you were in when it started. By the next Sunday you have a map, and the hardest work goes where the ones are.

When your hours belong to others

If you work thirty hours for rent or your family needs you home every weekend, find the ninety minutes a week that belong to you alone. That might be the hour before an early shift or a commute with a seat. Customer calls are the exception, because the people you call go home at five, so put those at a weekday lunch or the first hour of a day off. Logan, a Wake Forest founder in the book, spent his freshman year buried in Division I baseball. By sophomore year it settled. “Your baseline for what counts as enough time changes.”

Defend the deep work

Gloria Mark, a researcher at the University of California, Irvine, found that getting back to an interrupted task takes around twenty-three minutes. During your most important work, put the phone in another room. Face down on the desk doesn’t count, and neither does silent in your pocket.

Tell the truth to the adults whose job it is to catch you. Every time I told a professor, coach or advisor what was going on, I walked out with an extension, a referral or a name I didn’t have.
