How to choose a co-founder in college

I started Lucid Bots as a Davidson College junior with $42 in the company account. This is the short version of what my book, The Student Founder, says about it.

By Andrew Ashur, founder and CEO of Lucid Bots · November 19, 2026

Short answer

Build and ship one small project together before anyone talks about equity. Use those four weeks to check whether the other person shares your level of obsession, whether you can have the hardest conversation with them, and whether they make your thinking sharper. If any of the three comes back no, sometimes the right answer is one founder.

On a Sunday in December 2017, the weekend before finals, Adrian, David and I strapped a spray payload under an off-the-shelf drone with PVC pipe, zip ties and duct tape. None of us had signed anything. We ran the test this guide describes without knowing it. Most of the pairs who come to me run that order backwards. When two friends tell me they want to start something, my first question is always the same: what have you two finished together?

Define the job first

Write the job down in one sentence. What does this person own that you can’t? Extra hands can be hired later, for money. In my experience a co-founder is the second person who owns every failure. That person takes the call you can’t take, tells you the price is wrong before a customer does, and carries the week you can’t carry.

Why it’s hard to undo

You can let an employee go. A co-founder owns a piece of what you both built, and undoing that means lawyers, a cap table, and a company that stops moving while two former partners argue about what a year of work was worth. A wrong customer costs you a month. A wrong partner can cost you the company and the friendship in the same quarter.

Two terms on the paper you sign decide most of it. Vesting means a share is earned over time, usually four years. A cliff means nothing is earned until a set date, usually the first anniversary, so a co-founder who leaves in month seven leaves with nothing.

Use the college years

Paul Graham wrote, “Most students don’t realize how rich they are in the scarcest ingredient in startups, co-founders.” On campus you live among people with real talent and no mortgages. Graham’s screening rule is to “look for the people who keep starting projects, and finish at least some of them.”

Spend these years running small things with many people before you commit to one. If you commute and work, look at the person you already split a shift with, or the one who did the work in the group project when nobody was watching.

Three questions

All three have to come back yes:

  • Do they share your level of obsession?
  • Are you comfortable having the hardest possible conversation with them, about money, equity, or one of you doing less than the other?
  • Do they make your thinking sharper, or only more agreeable?

Nearly every startup death I’ve watched up close started with a breakup between the founders, and the money ran out afterward. The ones I could trace went back to a partnership formed by proximity, like a roommate or a best friend. Noam Wasserman, then a researcher at Harvard, reports a survey in which venture capitalists put roughly sixty-five percent of the failures in their portfolio companies down to problems in the management team.

Run a four-week test

Make the test physical. Build one small project together in four weeks and ship it to somebody who is neither of you. A working sign-up form used by one actual class counts, and so does a delivered order or a landing page that took money. While it runs, watch three things:

  • Obsession shows up as unasked-for work. Notice who rewrote a confusing question at eleven at night, and who was still there in week three.
  • Rehearse the hard conversation in week two. Tell them the part they own isn’t working and what it’s costing you, then ask what a fair split would look like while there is nothing to split.
  • Count the decisions where one of you changed the other’s mind with an argument. If you can’t name one in four weeks, you are both skipping the thinking.

If the project is half-finished at four weeks, that pair, on that work, doesn’t finish.

Look for different strengths

Thomas, a founder I mentored at Davidson, shut his company down partly because he was doing more of the work than the people around him. “Your team is your business marriage,” he said. His advice is to take time to know the person before you commit. The best partners are strong where you are weak. A partner who thinks like you doubles everything you both miss.

The three questions miss one test, and it applies to each of you alone. Can you see yourself doing this particular work, in this shape, for a decade? Hardly anyone I’ve watched asks it up front, and most of them find the answer years later, when one partner leaves.

Settle the split early

Before anybody shakes hands, agree on who is full-time and when, who brought the idea, who is putting in money, who owns the customer, and what happens if one of you leaves in month seven. I’d take fifty-fifty or fifty-five forty-five over a technically correct eighty-twenty that one of you quietly resents. Write the numbers down with four-year vesting and a one-year cliff, and email them to each other the day you agree.
