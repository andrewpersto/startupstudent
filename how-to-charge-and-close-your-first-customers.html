How to charge and close your first customers

I started Lucid Bots as a Davidson College junior with $42 in the company account. This is the short version of what my book, The Student Founder, says about it.

By Andrew Ashur, founder and CEO of Lucid Bots · November 19, 2026

Short answer

Charge from your first pilot, and set the price as a fraction of what the problem costs the customer. Say the number out loud and stop talking. Within an hour of a yes, send a five-line email with the success metric and a way to pay.

Charge from day one

Every industrial customer I’ve closed needed to see the product work on their own site first. The most important word in “paid pilot” is paid. Every free pilot I ran on a promise handed the customer permission to ignore me. Even a small setup fee of five hundred or a thousand dollars changes how seriously people take you.

I made one exception. A customer didn’t believe a drone could clean his parking deck, so I cleaned it myself, by hand, overnight. Do the work free once only when the work is the argument and you’ve decided in advance what a no costs you.

Define success up front: “Over thirty days we will clean ten thousand square feet, and if we cut your labor cost by twenty percent, the pilot is a success.” Write in that hitting the number converts the pilot into a full contract.

Price the value

The instinct is to add a margin to what the product cost you. Start in the discovery call instead. When a customer describes the pain, put a dollar figure on it together, out loud. By the end of a good call you should be able to say, “So this is costing you about eight thousand dollars a month,” and watch them nod.

Your price is a fraction of that. Save a cleaning company ten thousand dollars a month and charge something like three or four thousand. They keep more than half the savings.

When the buyer has no payroll, price against their next-best way out. For summer move-out storage, the alternative is a rental van twice and a weekend of hauling. Your number sits under that total and over what the job costs you in hours. In the book’s example, the price is eighty dollars for the summer.

Say the number

Say the price right after you’ve named what the problem costs: “This is costing you eight thousand a month. Our price is three.” Then stop talking.

When you hear “that’s too expensive,” don’t discount on the spot. In my deals, that objection meant the buyer didn’t yet see the value. Ask, “Help me understand. The problem is costing you eight thousand a month, and the fix is three. Where does that not add up for you?” If every number you name gets accepted without a flinch, raise the next one.

Follow up with something

On July 31, 2018, I emailed a nearby college’s facilities office about cleaning its buildings. The email had no specific ask, a slide show, two article links and an offer of “a great deal.” It got nothing back, not even a no. My rewrite opens on one line about the recipient’s building, says what we do in a sentence, and asks for fifteen minutes on site on Tuesday or Thursday morning.

Every follow-up has to carry value:

  • Day 1, the ask.
  • Day 4, an article or data point that proves you’re paying attention.
  • Day 10, a thirty-second video of the product working.
  • Day 20, a breakup email saying you’ll stop reaching out, with an easy way back in.

Never send an email that only says “checking in.” If you can’t think of something worth sharing, you don’t know the industry well enough yet.

Make the call

I was bad at sales, I hated it, and I didn’t like cold calling either. Send a short note first with the one thing you saw on their building, what you do, and that you’ll call in the next fifteen minutes. Then call inside fifteen minutes. My twenty-second opener names one problem they can walk outside and check, and ends on “Would you like an estimate to clean it?” If they say yes, send the estimate the same day.

The five objections

Most of the nos I’ve heard were the same five doubts, and most of them meant “I don’t yet believe this will work.” End every answer on a question.

  • “We already have someone.” Ask what it looks like when the current setup falls short, and find out when the contract is up.
  • “It feels risky.” Shrink the ask to one building, thirty days, paid.
  • “It’s too expensive.” Go back to the math.
  • “Send me some information.” Ask for the one question they’d need answered to take this seriously.
  • “Not right now.” Ask what would need to be true, then come back the week it happens.

The hour after yes

A yes is fragile. Inside the hour, send five lines: what you’ll do, by when, for how much, what you need to start, and the number that decides whether the pilot worked. Put the payment link or invoice in the same email. Write down their exact words for why they said yes, ask for an introduction, and put the first check-in on your calendar.
