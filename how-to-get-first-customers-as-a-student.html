How to get your first customers as a student

I started Lucid Bots as a Davidson College junior with $42 in the company account. This is the short version of what my book, The Student Founder, says about it.

By Andrew Ashur, founder and CEO of Lucid Bots · November 19, 2026

Short answer

Call people who have the problem as a student researching their industry, with nothing to sell yet. Ask about things that already happened, never about what they would buy. Narrow your market to fifty to two hundred people you can name, show them something that works, and don’t call it demand until strangers pay.

Start with research calls

The summer after I graduated, I wrote a Python script that pulled more than forty-five thousand names and phone numbers for exterior cleaning companies out of the Yellow Pages and started calling them from a folding table. Our drone was half-built, so I had nothing to sell. Most calls ended fast. About one in ten, a manager would sigh and start describing what the dangerous manual work was costing him.

Those calls changed what I thought the problem was. I had assumed building owners wanted speed. They wanted to stop carrying the liability. Cleaning companies wanted to stop losing the workers who could do the job.

Open every call by saying you are a student and you aren’t selling anything. On nearly every call I made, saying that took the defensiveness out of the conversation.

Questions that work

The book gives an eight-question script. These five do most of the work:

  • Walk me through a normal day, from when your team arrives to when they leave.
  • Which part of that would you hand to somebody else tomorrow?
  • What happens when that breaks?
  • What did it cost you the last time? Keep asking until you get dollars, hours or people.
  • How often does that happen?

Close with two asks: who else should I talk to, and can I come back and show you something rough? Don’t ask “Would you use this?” People know what they did last Tuesday and can only guess what they would do next spring. After each call, write down the biggest pain, the exact words they used for it, and what you would need to show them next.

Do the work yourself once

To win over a customer who didn’t believe a drone could clean his parking deck, I cleaned it myself by hand, from five in the evening to five the next morning, at thirty-four degrees. I burned my hand with the cleaning solution and still have the scars. After that night, cleaning company owners talked to me differently, because they could tell I had done the job.

The night also showed me something no phone call had. Crews lost hours rigging equipment and waiting for the wind before any cleaning started. Nobody mentioned it because it had always been that way. If I had only asked questions in an office, we would have built a product that cleaned faster. We built one that removed the setup.

Bring a demo

We got dozens of our earliest units into customers’ hands without a slide deck. We drove up to hotels with a drone in the truck, asked for the property manager, and asked for five minutes to show it working. Half the time the manager called a regional director before we had packed up. If you can’t bring the product, bring five before-and-after photos and a fifteen-second clip of it working.

Logan, who ran a parking app, printed flyers that looked like parking tickets and put them on badly parked cars. It cost the price of printing and reached people at the moment they had the problem.

Pick a micro-niche

Our market was industrial cleaning. Our micro-niche was commercial cleaning companies in windy coastal regions that relied on swing stages for tall buildings. That let us talk about wind and OSHA compliance on forty-story facades, and prospects saw people who understood their work. Narrow yours by four filters: where the pain is concentrated, who feels it hardest, what triggers it, and the word insiders use for it.

A niche is tight enough when you can name fifty to two hundred specific, reachable people in it who have the pain and the authority to say yes.

Know when it’s real

After thirty to fifty conversations, the book’s line for going ahead has three parts. At least a third of the people you spoke with describe the same pain in nearly the same words. At least one stranger has paid or put down a deposit. And the interest came from at least two sources you could run again next week.

The last part matters. Logan’s app got a flood of reservations one afternoon after a radio host mentioned it on air. Years later he called it “a little bit of false product market fit.” Write your results as a fraction. One yes from five conversations and one yes from fifty feel the same and mean different things.
