How to fund a student startup

I started Lucid Bots as a Davidson College junior with $42 in the company account. This is the short version of what my book, The Student Founder, says about it.

By Andrew Ashur, founder and CEO of Lucid Bots · November 19, 2026

Short answer

Decide whether to raise before you ask how. If your business can reach profitability on a few dozen customers, you probably shouldn’t raise at all. If your problem needs large amounts of capital before it earns a profit, find your first investors by asking for advice months before you ask for money.

Most of the founders I’ve watched raise money would have been better off if they had never raised it. Raising sells a piece of your future freedom and ownership for capital that, if you’re disciplined, buys speed.

Bootstrapping or raising

A lifestyle business exists to support your life. A venture-scale startup goes after a problem big enough to serve millions of people and usually needs money up front for research, hardware and hiring. Venture investors need every company to return many times their money, because a few enormous wins pay for a portfolio of failures.

Ana and I bootstrapped Persto, a platform for nonprofit mentorship programs, because we knew it could reach profitability on a few dozen customers. We own all of it, and it’s profitable. Lucid Bots needed world-class engineers, expensive prototypes and years of regulatory work. If we had bootstrapped it, I believe a better-funded competitor would have overtaken us inside eighteen months.

Thomas raised real money for a company that didn’t make it, and he points first at the raise. His advice to students now is to skip thinking about raising unless you’re building something like drones, and to ship something and find out if anyone wants it.

The default-alive math

Paul Graham calls the point where a business covers its founders’ living expenses ramen profitable. For a student, that might be six hundred dollars a month. His default-alive test asks whether, with expenses held flat and revenue growing at its current rate, you reach profitability on the money you have left. Run it with three numbers.

  • What’s in the account.
  • What leaves it in a month.
  • What comes in during a month.

Subtract the third from the second and divide the balance by the result to get your runway in months. If income won’t grow to cover spending in time, you can cut spending, grow revenue faster, or raise. Spending is the only one of the three you control by yourself.

Payback period

We once leased our drones instead of selling them, because we had absorbed the conventional wisdom that investors love recurring revenue. Every lease we celebrated was a fresh hole in our bank account. Take the all-in cost to deliver one unit and divide it by what the customer pays each month. A drone that costs two thousand dollars to deliver at one hundred a month takes twenty months to pay back. We flipped to selling outright in 2022, and the bank account stopped falling.

First investors

I didn’t know a single investor when I started. A mentor from a campus competition introduced me to an investor in Chicago, who became my first investor. A student fund or a pitch competition can write a first small check, and it asks you to be findable with something real to show.

The move I’ve gotten the most from is advice-first outreach. I asked operators in Charlotte for fifteen minutes and one piece of advice, went and acted on it, and came back to show them what happened. Months later, when we opened a small round, I offered them the first chance to join, and most said yes before I finished the sentence.

Keep those people close with a short update every two weeks or once a month. Say what you did, what’s next and where you could use help, and send the bad ones on time too. Some of our best checks came from people who had quietly read our updates for half a year.

Dilution and SAFEs

The appendix recommends a SAFE for your first money, an instrument Y Combinator built so founders can raise without negotiating a valuation. Track total dilution across every SAFE at once. Three small SAFEs that each feel like ten percent can add up to roughly thirty percent of your company before a new lead takes their twenty. My rule of thumb for students is to expect a seed round to cost around twenty percent.

One of our investors, who has spent two decades in venture, told me he has found it easier to remove a co-founder than an investor.

Y Combinator

We applied to YC twice and didn’t get an interview the first time. In 2019 a video of an ugly drone that flew got us in. YC funds companies with no product and no users, and founders it turned down the year before. Read YC’s own pages the week you apply, because they change.

After the money lands

A dangerous window opens about forty-eight hours after the wire hits, and it is where I’ve watched funded startups begin to die. The ones that died started spending like companies ten times their size. If you were building in a garage, stay there until the inventory won’t fit.
