How to start a business in college

I started Lucid Bots as a Davidson College junior with $42 in the company account. This is the short version of what my book, The Student Founder, says about it.

By Andrew Ashur, founder and CEO of Lucid Bots · November 19, 2026

Short answer

Start with a problem you have seen up close, and treat it as a project before it is a company. Write down how much money and how many months you are willing to spend before you spend any. Then talk to the people who have the problem, show them something rough, and ask them to pay. Being a student makes each of those steps cheaper than it will be after you graduate.

Why college is the cheapest time to try

While you are a student, a wrong guess costs you a semester. After graduation it costs a salary you have come to depend on, a lease, and years you can’t get back.

Paul Graham’s advice is “Do not start a startup in college.” His strongest argument is that a twenty-year-old can’t yet tell a good idea from a bad one. I agree about the judgment and disagree about where it comes from. You build judgment by watching an idea you were sure of meet a real customer and come back wrong, and you can afford more of those lessons on campus than anywhere else. Y Combinator, the accelerator Graham co-founded, later funded my company.

Being a student also gets you meetings. When I said “I’m a student,” facility managers, professors and alumni took my calls. Some of the same desks closed to me the week after I graduated.

What happens if it fails

Most students I talk to care less about the upside than about this. Gustavo Manso, an economist at Berkeley, studied founders whose companies failed and found that they tend to return to paid work and recover their old earnings within a few years.

The book follows two of them. Thomas raised about $200,000 for a tool that wrote contracts for musicians, shut it down with money still in the bank, and now helps run a working musician’s business because of what the startup taught him. Logan ran a parking marketplace with his father for four years. When it closed, he says the company he had built is what got him hired at his next job. Both lost money and time, and the chapter is honest about what that cost them.

Set your stop line first

Before the first dollar goes out, write down two numbers: the most money you are willing to lose and the most months you are willing to spend. I call that pair your stop line. Mine were about $15,000 and a year and a half.

To size the months, pick the point where you would resent the time spent, then subtract one. To size the dollars, pick an amount you could lose this semester and still make rent. Then list what the money will buy. If the list costs more than your limit, cut the list.

If you can’t afford to lose any money, bet hours instead. Keep the job and the grades, and use the four or five hours a week nobody else is getting from you. Daymond John waited tables at Red Lobster for years while he built FUBU.

Start with a project

You don’t need an LLC on day one. In the book, a project becomes a company on the day one of three things happens: someone signs a pilot, someone pays you for a job, or you build something that could hurt a person. Until then, it’s something you work on at nine at night, which is how my co-founders and I ran it in our junior year.

Two rules that keep it alive

The dead companies I have seen up close got one of two things wrong. The first is cash. Know how many months of runway you have and what will pay for the month after that. The second is revenue. You should be able to name one person who has the problem, the smallest thing you could sell them this month, and the price. Count only money that moves. Pledges and excited texts don’t count.

What to do this week

Take your Agency Quotient: count the days between having an idea and doing the first thing about it that a stranger could react to. Then pick one problem you keep noticing, find one person who works inside it, and ask them a question you can’t answer from the internet. The next two guides cover finding the idea and finding the first customers.
