How to build your first product as a student

I started Lucid Bots as a Davidson College junior with $42 in the company account. This is the short version of what my book, The Student Founder, says about it.

By Andrew Ashur, founder and CEO of Lucid Bots · November 19, 2026

Short answer

Build the smallest thing that gets a real reaction from a real person, then stop and watch. Before you write any code, deliver the result by hand to your first few customers. If your first version will take more than about four weeks, you’ve probably scoped it wrong.

Learn it yourself

We tried to buy our way past the hard part. We bought DJI drones and strapped a cleaning system to them. When the hardware got heavy enough for real buildings, we paid thirty thousand dollars for an eight-motor model. After a firmware update, it counted six motors and never flew again. In December 2018 our last drone lost a motor during a demo at NC State and broke apart on the concrete in front of one of the largest cleaning companies in the area.

That Christmas my uncle gave me a book called DIY Drones with Arduino. I was an economics and Spanish major who could not read a wiring diagram. I started treating “I’m not technical” as a decision, and I made it the other way with a twelve-dollar soldering iron and a wall of YouTube tabs. Thirty-one days in, my drone shot a hundred feet up over Davidson’s intramural field and came back down without breaking.

Fake it first

Before I built a single autonomous drone, I was the autonomy. The customer bought a clean building, and I delivered it by hand. The lean-startup world calls this a concierge MVP.

Say your idea is software that builds a weekly report. Make it by hand in a spreadsheet, email it every Friday, and watch whether people complain the week you’re late. Find the one mechanism that creates the value, deliver it by the crudest means that works, and build the real machine once people are pulling that value out of your hands.

Ship it ugly

In 2019 we ran our first paid job on a drone we had built ourselves. It was held together with zip ties and duct tape, and half the parts were 3D-printed in whatever filament I had that week. When we finished, the facility manager was looking at his building, cleaner than his old crew had ever gotten it, done faster, at a lower price than he was used to paying.

Write three questions in your customer’s units and answer them the week after you deliver. Is the result better? Did it cost less? Did it take less time? If you can’t put a number in each blank, you don’t know yet what you delivered.

Speed and learning

Iterate once a month and you get twelve tries a year. Iterate daily and you get three hundred and sixty-five. I’ll take my version twenty over your well-planned version one, because version twenty has met nineteen real people.

AI tools now let you build faster than you can learn. A version two built before you’ve watched anyone use version one is two guesses in a row.

Watch it once

The book walks one idea, an app that matches students into study groups, through four steps:

  • Write the logic map first, one paragraph in plain English covering input, transformation, output and edge cases.
  • Run it by hand. Post a free form in three group chats, sort the responses yourself that night, and text each group. If twenty sign up and fifteen show up, you have proof.
  • Build the first real version with an AI assistant. Tell it you are a non-engineer, ask for the simplest stack and the screens you need, and build one screen at a time.
  • Hand the rough version to five students and keep quiet while they use it. Their confusion is your next task list.

Test without talking

Skip the survey. In my experience a survey collects the polite answer. Sit a real person down, hand them the thing, say nothing, and watch for the moment they get stuck. When someone rigs a workaround, they’re showing you what to build next. Ask the next person before you build for the last one.

What to hand AI

Hand the tool the labor, such as the code, the first draft and the research. Keep the thinking for yourself. In my experience it is good at the probable and weak at the exact, so the price on a quote, a count and whether the code runs are yours to check. When I don’t trust its count of a building’s stories, I pull up Street View and count them myself.

We once tried a drone a crew could talk to. Holding three meters off a wall is an exact job, and a distance sensor running plain code does it the same way every time, so asking a model to do it was a bad experiment. Now we ask whether a job needs AI at all before we reach for it. When the assistant hands you code, ask it to explain each block line by line until you can explain it yourself.
