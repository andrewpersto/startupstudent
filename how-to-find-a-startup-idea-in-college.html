How to find a startup idea in college

I started Lucid Bots as a Davidson College junior with $42 in the company account. This is the short version of what my book, The Student Founder, says about it.

By Andrew Ashur, founder and CEO of Lucid Bots · November 19, 2026

Short answer

You find ideas by watching people do work that costs them money, time or safety. They rarely arrive in a flash. Keep a log of frustrations for one week, keep the ones that repeat and cost someone something, and test the survivors by asking the people who have the problem for a commitment instead of an opinion.

I found mine in the fall of my junior year, after watching window washers work on a building in the wind. I spent months reading safety filings and trade forums and calling strangers before I knew what I was looking at. The students I meet at office hours often wait for a lightning bolt, and when it doesn’t come they decide they aren’t the creative ones.

Look at boring industries

The Small Business Administration counts more than thirty-six million small businesses in the United States, employing close to half the private workforce. Cleaning companies, logistics yards and repair shops rarely show up in a case competition, and in my experience the boring problems have had far less competition than the exciting ones.

My friend Jack started out cleaning boats for a dealership at twelve dollars an hour. Then he priced the same work at his parents’ lake, where nobody detailed boats at the dock: about $2,000 a boat for four or five hours of work. The company he built from that dock sold with two competing offers inside a month.

One quick test comes from Marc Andreessen. Look up the leading companies in an industry and check whether their founders still run them. If they do, the industry is young. If generations of caretakers run it, the industry has settled into habit, and a newcomer with a better tool can get in.

Vitamin or painkiller

Investors sort ideas with one question. A vitamin is nice to have, and nobody would miss it for long. A painkiller fixes an urgent, expensive problem. Most campus ideas I hear are vitamins, and vitamins are the first thing cut when budgets tighten. Before you go further, you want a yes to these:

  • Is someone losing real money, time or safety because of this problem right now?
  • Would they pay to make it stop before your product is finished?
  • If your solution disappeared overnight, would they be in real trouble?

The first two need a yes now. The third gets answered later, by real users.

Keep a friction log

For one week, write down three frustrations a day, yours and other people’s, in the words the person used. On Sunday, cross out anything that costs nobody money, time or safety, and anything that won’t happen again. Rank what is left by how often it happens and take the top three into the questions above.

Listen for sentences like “I can’t believe I have to do this again.” Look for the clipboard in a multimillion-dollar business, the thirty-tab spreadsheet one person understands, and anyone doing dangerous or repetitive work because nobody has built a better tool.

Use the job you already have

If you work a job you need, you already have the inside view I had to pay for. On the floor, ask three questions: what part of this job would you pay never to do again, what breaks most, and what do you do when it breaks? If your workplace bills customers for your hours, compare its rate with your wage. Before you build anything from a work problem, check what you signed when you were hired.

Being an outsider helps

When I started, I had never built a drone, flown one or pressure washed a building. Because nobody had trained me in the right way to clean a building, I kept asking, “Why does it have to be done that way?” Sometimes the answer was a real constraint. Usually it was habit.

Pair that with insider knowledge from three sources: the public record nobody reads (mine was OSHA filings), the forum where people in the trade complain to each other, and five ten-minute phone calls to practitioners who don’t know you.

Ask for commitments

Rob Fitzpatrick’s The Mom Test is about the polite lies people tell when you ask whether your idea is good. Ask for something that costs them instead. An hour of their time is the first rung. An introduction to their boss is the second, because it puts their reputation behind you. A deposit or a payment from a stranger is the third. If you can’t get any of the three, the idea isn’t working, however well the pitch goes.

Is it the right idea for you?

The book asks about interest, access and capacity. To test interest, count the days since the problem first bothered you and check whether anything exists yet because of it. For access, you need one way in, and being a student counts. For capacity, ask whether you can build or learn to build an ugly first version that works. Then ask yourself why five times. If the last answer is money or status, keep digging or change the idea.
