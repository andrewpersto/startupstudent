How to market a student startup

I started Lucid Bots as a Davidson College junior with $42 in the company account. This is the short version of what my book, The Student Founder, says about it.

By Andrew Ashur, founder and CEO of Lucid Bots · November 19, 2026

Short answer

Make something worth watching and let people watch you make it. Post your work as you do it, three times a week, in the one place your customers already gather. Give it ninety days before you judge it, and count the posts you wrote before you count who saw them.

A customer once told me the best salesperson his company ever hired was a robot. When his crew flew our drone up the side of a building, people on the sidewalk stopped to watch, and a few handed over business cards before the drone had landed.

Marketing as showing

The students I talk to have a quiet contempt for marketing, because a lot of it paints over a product to make people want it. The attention that lasts comes from showing people, plainly and often, something true they didn’t know existed. In my view, the shame is in building something good and letting it die in silence because you were too proud or too scared to point at it.

Build in public

Talk about your work the whole way, while it’s still ugly and still breaking. Founders online call this building in public. You are already failing, fixing and shipping, so let people watch. Post the thing you taught yourself this week, the prototype you broke, or the one thing a brutal customer call changed.

Film far more than feels reasonable early on, because you can’t go back later and capture the garage or the first broken prototype. The book’s post template is four lines and an image:

  • What you did or what broke.
  • Why it surprised you.
  • What it taught you.
  • What’s next.

The fear of theft

At first we guarded the idea, because we thought someone smarter and better funded would hear it and build it first. Each time we did describe it, we got better at saying it, and the pushback we got was free consulting. The introduction, the first customer and the first check all arrived because somebody knew what we were building.

My first posts

I had fewer than five hundred LinkedIn connections, and I didn’t like the sound of my own voice. My first post announced the company and got thirty-two likes and zero comments. Nobody stole the idea.

Later, one of our earliest customers was pitching Davidson College on his own cleaning services when Davidson mentioned a student company already cleaning buildings with drones. He assumed it was a joke. He went home, googled “Davidson College drone cleaning,” found that post and my first rough YouTube video, and asked for a meeting.

It took me years to reach eleven thousand LinkedIn followers. In August 2026 I posted a photo on X of a teammate in our Charlotte shop testing circuit boards we designed, and it crossed a million views in a day. It was luck, and the luck needed the boards, the company and years of unremarkable posts to exist first.

Pick one place

Choose the single place where the people you care about already gather. For me, building an industrial company, that was a professional network. If your customers are in one place and the founders you admire are in another, pick the customers. Post three times a week. Write one after a work session, one after a conversation and one after something breaks.

Two measurements

Write these down before you start, because the version of you in week three will want to move them.

  • Day 30: a dozen posts up, each one quicker to write than the last.
  • Day 90: one real-world result from your first forty posts, such as a message from a stranger, a lead, or someone who arrives already knowing your story.

Don’t change platforms before day ninety, because before then a quiet feed tells you nothing. My tenth post did almost nothing, and it is part of why my fiftieth landed.

Boring work counts

All of this assumes the product underneath is real, and no amount of posting saves one nobody needs. Ali Abdaal was a medical student at Cambridge when he started posting videos about how he revised for exams. He made more than fifty videos in his first six months and had barely a thousand subscribers. Within a few years his channel passed a million.

What it costs

Keep at it and you own a distribution channel. Ours brought us customers, co-workers who had watched us grow for months, and investors who walked into the first meeting feeling they knew us. It also showed us that something like two-thirds of our website leads came from other countries. The cost in dollars is close to zero, and fifteen minutes after a shift is enough to write one honest line about your day.
